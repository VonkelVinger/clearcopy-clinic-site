# Headline Clinic: Educational Logic Specification

## 1. Purpose and educational stance

Headline Clinic version one helps journalism students diagnose and improve headlines they have written. Its purpose is to strengthen a student's editorial judgement: identifying what a headline gets right, where it loses accuracy or impact, and what the student should reconsider.

It must not simply replace the student's work with an AI-generated headline. The primary response should be diagnosis and targeted coaching questions. Example headlines are a later learning aid, not the first answer.

## 2. Ratings

Each assessment category receives one of three ratings.

| Rating | Meaning | Expected response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Strong** | The headline is well supported by the story and serves the category effectively. | Name the strength and, where useful, offer a small refinement question. |
| **Needs attention** | The headline has a fixable weakness, omission, ambiguity, or mismatch with the story. | Explain the issue using story evidence and ask a focused revision question. |
| **Serious problem** | The headline risks materially misleading readers, unfairly harming a person or group, or making an unsupported claim. | Give a clear warning, identify the problem, and require reconsideration before suggesting refinement. |

The overall diagnosis should distinguish between an imperfect headline and one that must not be published in its current form.

## 3. Assessment categories

### Accuracy

**What a strong headline does**

- States only what the story establishes.
- Represents the central event, finding, or development without exaggeration.
- Uses a level of certainty that matches the evidence.

**Common student mistakes**

- Claiming a cause when the story only shows correlation or timing.
- Turning an allegation, proposal, forecast, or preliminary finding into a confirmed fact.
- Adding a number, location, actor, or outcome not supported by the story.
- Overstating scale with words such as “all,” “ban,” “crisis,” or “proves.”

**Evidence required from the pasted story**

- The headline's subject, action, outcome, and any numerical claim must appear in, or follow directly from, the story.
- The story must support the headline's level of certainty and timeframe.

**Useful coaching feedback**

- “The story says the council *proposed* the change. Does ‘introduces’ make the decision sound final?”
- “Which sentence supports the word ‘record’? If none does, what more precise description could you use?”
- “The study found an association. How could the headline avoid implying that it proved a cause?”

**Serious-warning triggers**

- The headline asserts a fact contradicted by the story.
- It presents an allegation as established fact.
- It invents a material detail, outcome, or causal claim.
- It creates a false impression that an event has happened when it is only proposed, expected, or alleged.

### Clarity

**What a strong headline does**

- Can be understood quickly on its own.
- Makes clear who did what, or what changed, without unnecessary ambiguity.
- Uses familiar, direct language suited to the audience.

**Common student mistakes**

- Ambiguous pronouns, dangling modifiers, or unclear relationships between people and actions.
- Dense jargon, unexplained acronyms, or too many ideas in one line.
- Wordplay that obscures the main news.
- A headline that is grammatically correct but can be read in more than one important way.

**Evidence required from the pasted story**

- The story must identify the relevant actors, action, and context needed to test whether a reasonable reader can interpret the headline correctly.

**Useful coaching feedback**

- “Who does ‘they’ refer to in this headline? Can you name the actor?”
- “A reader who has not seen the story may not know this acronym. Is there a plainer term?”
- “Does the first phrase tell the reader what has actually happened?”

**Serious-warning triggers**

- Ambiguity could lead a reader to identify the wrong person, group, action, or outcome.
- The wording materially reverses or confuses responsibility.
- The headline is so unclear that its main claim cannot be reliably understood.

### Specificity

**What a strong headline does**

- Identifies the most informative verified subject, action, place, or consequence.
- Gives readers enough detail to distinguish this story from a generic one.
- Selects details that matter, rather than merely adding length.

**Common student mistakes**

- Vague labels such as “issue,” “move,” “change,” or “concerns” when the story provides a specific action or outcome.
- Omitting the actor or affected group when it is central to the news.
- Using a broad claim when the story's scope is limited.
- Stuffing in details that do not advance the headline's meaning.

**Evidence required from the pasted story**

- The story must contain the specific subject, action, location, population, figure, or consequence used in the headline.
- Scope must match the story: local is not national, some is not all, and one case is not a trend.

**Useful coaching feedback**

- “What is the specific change in the story? Could you replace ‘new plan’ with that action?”
- “The story names the affected neighbourhood. Would including it help readers understand why this matters?”
- “Does ‘students’ describe everyone affected, or only one group mentioned in the story?”

**Serious-warning triggers**

- The headline expands a limited finding or event into a broad claim.
- It leaves out a limiting detail in a way that changes the meaning.
- It applies a label to an individual or group that the story does not support.

### News value

**What a strong headline does**

- Leads with the most timely, consequential, novel, relevant, or useful verified element.
- Reflects the story's real angle rather than a minor detail.
- Helps the intended audience see why the story matters.

**Common student mistakes**

- Leading with background, process, ceremony, or a colourful quote instead of the development.
- Treating a routine event as a major breakthrough.
- Hiding the consequence or public relevance.
- Using click-driven drama where the story's real value is service, accountability, or community relevance.

**Evidence required from the pasted story**

- The story must indicate what is new, what changed, who is affected, and why it matters now.
- The tool should not infer significance that the pasted facts cannot support.

**Useful coaching feedback**

- “What is new today: the meeting, the decision, or the effect on residents?”
- “The story's strongest verified consequence is the closure date. Could that be more useful to lead with?”
- “Is this quote the news, or does it support a more important development?”

**Serious-warning triggers**

- The headline manufactures urgency, public danger, or significance absent from the story.
- It uses sensational framing that materially distorts the news value.
- It suppresses a central public consequence in a way that makes the headline misleading.

### Style

**What a strong headline does**

- Fits the selected story type and the likely audience.
- Is concise, active, and readable without sacrificing accuracy.
- Uses an appropriate tone: informational for hard news, interpretive but fair for opinion, inviting but truthful for features, and platform-aware for digital or social headlines.

**Common student mistakes**

- Using promotional, emotional, or pun-heavy language for hard news.
- Writing an opinion headline as though it were neutral reporting.
- Using vague clickbait for digital distribution.
- Making a feature headline so literary that readers cannot tell what the story concerns.

**Evidence required from the pasted story**

- The story's type, genre cues, angle, and audience-relevant context must support the chosen tone and wording.
- Style choices must not conceal a fact the story requires the reader to understand.

**Useful coaching feedback**

- “For hard news, would a direct verb serve the reader better than this pun?”
- “Because this is opinion, can the headline signal that it is an argument rather than a reported finding?”
- “What useful keyword might a reader search for when looking for this digital story?”

**Serious-warning triggers**

- Style makes an opinion, advertisement, allegation, or satire appear to be verified news.
- Clickbait wording creates a materially false expectation about the story.
- The tone is demeaning, discriminatory, or needlessly inflammatory.

### Fairness and risk

**What a strong headline does**

- Uses fair, proportionate language, especially about individuals and vulnerable groups.
- Makes clear whether a claim is alleged, disputed, charged, found, or confirmed.
- Avoids unnecessary identification, stigma, and foreseeable harm.

**Common student mistakes**

- Naming or labelling someone more strongly than the story supports.
- Omitting “alleged,” “according to,” or other necessary attribution.
- Treating one person's account as settled fact.
- Identifying people unnecessarily in sensitive cases.
- Using stereotypes or language that reduces people to a condition, identity, or accusation.

**Evidence required from the pasted story**

- The story must show the source and status of claims, relevant denials or uncertainty, legal stage, and any details needed to judge whether identification is justified.

**Useful coaching feedback**

- “Is the wrongdoing established in the story, or is it an allegation that needs attribution?”
- “Does the story explain why this person needs to be identified in the headline?”
- “Could this wording unfairly define a person by an accusation or characteristic?”

**Serious-warning triggers**

- Defamatory, accusatory, discriminatory, or stigmatizing language not supported by the story.
- An allegation presented as established fact.
- Unnecessary identification in a sensitive case.
- A headline that creates a foreseeable safety, legal, or privacy risk.

## 4. Student workflow

1. The student submits a story or story summary and their proposed headline. They may also select a story type. In the underlying system, this input is a submission; version one's interface user is always a student.
2. The tool diagnoses the proposed headline against all six assessment categories, using only the pasted story as evidence.
3. The tool asks targeted improvement questions rather than writing a replacement headline. Questions should focus on the highest-priority weaknesses first.
4. The student revises their headline.
5. The tool compares the first and revised attempts, identifying which issues improved, which remain, and what trade-offs the revision created.
6. Only after the student has revised may the tool offer limited example headlines as learning aids. Each example must be clearly labelled as an example, tied to the pasted story, and accompanied by an explanation of the editorial choice it demonstrates.

The tool should reward thoughtful revision even when the revised headline is not yet strong. It should never frame its own wording as the one correct answer.

## 5. Optional published headline

When a student provides an optional published headline as part of a submission:

- Assess it independently using the same six categories and the same pasted story.
- Do not assume that it is correct, authoritative, or superior because it was published.
- Compare the student's headline and the published headline against the story, not primarily against one another.
- Explain differences as editorial choices and trade-offs where possible. A published headline may be stronger in one category and weaker in another.
- Do not encourage imitation without analysis.

## 6. Safeguards and boundaries

- Never invent facts, names, numbers, motives, causes, outcomes, or context not contained in the pasted story.
- Distinguish allegations from established facts. Preserve attribution, uncertainty, and legal status where the story requires them.
- Avoid presenting subjective judgments, predictions, or opinions as certainty.
- Warn students not to paste confidential, identifying, embargoed, or unpublished source material. Encourage a generalised summary when appropriate.
- In the first version, do not retain pasted copy, student headlines, or published headlines after the active session or request is complete.
- If the material indicates immediate safety, privacy, legal, or defamation risk, prioritise a serious warning over ordinary style coaching.

## 7. Future extensibility

Journalism students are the primary users for version one. The current interface, coaching language, revision sequence, and example-headline restriction must remain focused on their learning.

The underlying assessment framework should nevertheless treat a person using the system as a **user** and their pasted story and headlines as a **submission**; it should not be technically hard-coded exclusively to student users. This supports possible future modes without making the first version broad or generic:

- Student coaching
- Lecturer or assessor review
- Working journalist self-review
- Editor or newsroom quality assurance

The same core categories may be used across these modes, but feedback tone, depth, workflow, and available actions may differ. For example, a lecturer mode could support assessment-oriented comments, while an editor mode could support quality-assurance checks. Those capabilities are out of scope for version one.

Future expansion must not weaken Headline Clinic's educational purpose or introduce professional newsroom complexity into version one.

## 8. Fictional test cases

### Test case 1: Proposal mistaken for a completed decision

**Story facts:** A city council committee recommends closing two libraries next year. The full council will vote next month. Residents have begun a petition against the proposal.

**Weak student headline:** “City closes two libraries”

**Expected diagnosis:** Serious problem for accuracy and fairness/risk. The closure is only a recommendation; the headline says it has already happened. Needs attention for specificity because it omits the committee and pending vote.

**Better direction for revision:** Reflect the proposal and decision stage, then consider the affected libraries or pending vote.

### Test case 2: Correlation presented as proof

**Story facts:** A university survey finds that students who sleep more report higher average marks. Researchers say the survey cannot show that sleep caused the difference.

**Weak student headline:** “More sleep proves better grades”

**Expected diagnosis:** Serious problem for accuracy. The story reports an association, not proof of causation. Needs attention for style if the wording is more absolute than the research warrants.

**Better direction for revision:** Use language that reflects a reported link or association and identify the survey population if space allows.

### Test case 3: The news buried under ceremony

**Story facts:** A community health clinic opens a weekend vaccination service after local measles cases rise. The launch included speeches by municipal officials.

**Weak student headline:** “Officials welcome clinic visitors at Saturday event”

**Expected diagnosis:** Needs attention for news value and specificity. The headline leads with the ceremony rather than the new weekend service and the public-health reason for it.

**Better direction for revision:** Lead with the service change, then decide whether the measles context is necessary and sufficiently supported.

### Test case 4: Ambiguity changes responsibility

**Story facts:** A school principal suspended a teacher after receiving a misconduct complaint. The teacher denies the allegation; an investigation is under way.

**Weak student headline:** “Teacher suspended for misconduct”

**Expected diagnosis:** Serious problem for fairness and risk. The wording implies misconduct is established and omits the allegation, denial, and investigation. Needs attention for accuracy.

**Better direction for revision:** Make the complaint and the unresolved status clear, while avoiding unnecessary detail or identification.

### Test case 5: Feature tone obscures the subject

**Story facts:** A feature follows three residents who use a newly restored river path for commuting, exercise, and social connection. The path reopened after flood repairs.

**Weak student headline:** “A ribbon of hope winds through the city”

**Expected diagnosis:** Needs attention for clarity and specificity. The image may suit a feature, but readers cannot tell that the story is about a restored river path or how residents use it.

**Better direction for revision:** Preserve some feature voice if desired, but include a concrete reference to the path, its reopening, or the people whose daily routines it affects.
